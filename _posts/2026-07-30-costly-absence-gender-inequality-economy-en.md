The debate over women's rights has changed substantially in recent decades. Earlier discussions often concentrated on minimum protections and the removal of explicit discrimination. Today, business and the economy have become central to the debate. In this context, gender inequality is both a violation of a fundamental right and a structural factor that weakens sustainable development, corporate governance, and market efficiency.

Women's economic empowerment is no longer framed solely as an ethical or social demand. It is increasingly understood as a legal and economic necessity. From a human-rights perspective, equal participation in economic life is inseparable from human dignity. International instruments, particularly the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW), require states to identify and remove legal and structural barriers to women's economic participation.

The central difficulty, however, lies in implementation, where the distance between formal equality and substantive equality becomes visible. Rules that appear neutral do not necessarily produce equal outcomes, especially when market and corporate structures reproduce existing inequalities.

Statistical evidence illustrates this divide. Reports by institutions including the World Bank and the World Economic Forum show that women's economic participation remains significantly below men's in most countries, and that the gap is not closing quickly even in advanced economies. The importance of these figures lies in their legal and economic consequences. Excluding or marginalising half of a society's human capital means accepting an incomplete market and an inefficient legal system.

Corporate and business law therefore have a central role. Corporations are not neutral economic units. They are legal institutions whose decisions about recruitment, promotion, pay, and board composition directly affect the distribution of opportunity and power.

Comparative research has associated gender diversity in corporate leadership with stronger performance in areas such as financial oversight, risk management, and governance transparency. Several legal systems, particularly in Europe, have attempted to narrow the gap through active legislative intervention. Mandatory or semi-mandatory rules concerning women's representation on the boards of large companies are one example.

These policies were initially criticised as inconsistent with merit-based selection. Experience has nevertheless shown that well-designed interventions need not weaken decision-making. By broadening the range of perspectives within an institution, they can reduce groupthink and improve accountability. Law can therefore perform a corrective function when its design responds to the institutional causes of inequality.

From the perspective of economic law, women's empowerment is also connected to growth and productivity. Development economists have repeatedly examined how higher female economic participation can expand productive capacity. In this context, law is more than a protective instrument for groups that have been marginalised. It is part of the institutional infrastructure necessary for a market to function effectively. Ignoring this relationship reduces law to abstract rules detached from economic conditions.

The argument should not be simplified. Women's empowerment in business cannot be achieved through symbolic appointments or performative policies. If labour laws do not provide workable forms of flexibility, if access to credit and financial resources remains structurally unequal, and if care responsibilities continue to fall disproportionately on women, formal equality will not produce meaningful results.

This is where substantive equality matters. Rather than requiring identical treatment in every case, it directs attention to the removal of structural barriers. Courts and regulatory institutions also have a role: anti-discrimination principles can be interpreted in light of actual inequality; claims concerning discrimination in employment can receive effective protection; and corporations can be required or encouraged to disclose their equality and diversity practices. Through these measures, law can influence market norms instead of merely recording them.

Women's economic empowerment should therefore be understood as part of the broader legal questions of the rule of law, accountable governance, and sustainable development. The question is no longer whether gender equality is desirable. It is how much economic and institutional loss legal systems are prepared to tolerate by allowing inequality to continue. In an economy organised around competition, innovation, and efficiency, disregarding women's capacity is a costly departure from legal and economic rationality.
